**Ghatasthapana** (*ghata*, pot; *sthapana*, installation), also called **kalash sthapana**, is the ritual that opens [Navratri](/navratri/). On the first day, Pratipada, a pot filled with water is installed and worshipped as the seat of the Goddess for the next nine days. In many homes it stands over a tray of soil sown with **barley (jau)**, which sprouts green by the end of the festival. In 2026, Navratri begins after **Mahalaya on Saturday 10 October**; check your local panchang for the exact first day and the auspicious window.

## What the kalash represents

A kalash filled with water, crowned with mango leaves and a coconut, is one of the oldest auspicious symbols in Hindu worship. The water stands for life and purity, the leaves for growth, and the coconut for the divine presence. A well-known verse describes the kalash as holding all the gods:

> कलशस्य मुखे विष्णुः कण्ठे रुद्रः समाश्रितः। मूले तत्र स्थितो ब्रह्मा मध्ये मातृगणाः स्मृताः॥
> Kalashasya mukhe vishnuh kanthe rudrah samashritah, mule tatra sthito brahma madhye matriganah smritah.
> Vishnu dwells at the kalash's mouth, Rudra at its neck, Brahma at its base, and the Mothers in its middle.

The barley sown around it is a sign of prosperity, and watching the sprouts grow gives the nine days a living rhythm. Bengali families use a very similar pot, called a **ghat**, in their own [Durga Puja at home](/durga-puja/at-home/), usually seated on a small heap of earth or rice.

## Choosing the muhurta

Ghatasthapana is done on Pratipada, the first lunar day of the bright fortnight of Ashvin. Because a tithi starts at different clock times in different places, the right time in Kolkata may not be the right time in Ahmedabad, Delhi or London. Panchangs typically give:

- the start and end of Pratipada for your location;
- a preferred **ghatasthapana muhurta**, often in the morning;
- a fallback window if the morning one can't be used;
- any periods traditionally avoided, per your panchang.

We deliberately don't print a time here. Use your local panchang, a trusted temple calendar or your family priest.

## Supplies

Gather everything the evening before: the soil tray, barley, kalash, mango leaves, coconut, red cloth and thread, roli, rice, lamp and flowers. The supplies list above covers the full set, and our [samagri list](/durga-puja/samagri-list/) adds items for the whole festival.

Tips:

- **Soil:** use clean garden soil; mixing in some sand helps drainage.
- **Barley:** soaking seeds for a few hours speeds sprouting. Sow thickly for a green carpet.
- **Coconut:** choose one with its husk. Many families keep the tufted end up; follow your custom.
- **Lamp:** if you plan an *akhand jyoti* (continuous flame), use a large lamp with a glass cover, keep it away from curtains, and never leave it where children or pets can knock it over.

## How to do it, step by step

1. Clean the space, set a low stool with red cloth, and place the Goddess's picture.
2. Spread soil in the tray, sow barley, cover lightly and dampen.
3. Draw a swastika on the kalash, tie red thread, fill with water and add a coin, betel nut and a few grains.
4. Put five mango leaves in the mouth; place the cloth-wrapped coconut on top.
5. Set the kalash in or beside the barley tray.
6. Light the lamp. Make your sankalpa: in your own words, say who you are, that it is the first day of Navratri, and that you will worship the Goddess for nine days.
7. Invite the Goddess into the kalash, offer roli, rice, flowers, incense, lamp and food, and perform aarti.
8. A simple mantra many people use throughout is:

> ॐ दुं दुर्गायै नमः
> Om Dum Durgayai Namah
> Salutations to Durga.

On the same day, many people also begin worshipping [Shailaputri](/navratri/navadurga/), the first of the nine forms.

## During the nine days

- Worship morning and evening; read a portion of the *Durga Saptashati* if you can.
- Water the barley lightly every day.
- Keep the area clean; don't move the kalash.
- Many families follow a [Navratri fast](/navratri/fasting-rules/) and end the festival with [kanya pujan](/navratri/kanya-pujan/).

## Closing the ritual

On Navami or [Vijayadashami](/navratri/dussehra-vijayadashami/), depending on family custom, do a final puja and say goodbye to the Goddess. Lift the coconut and leaves respectfully; the coconut is often broken and shared as prasad. Sprinkle the kalash water through the home and pour the rest into plants. The barley sprouts (called *jamara* or *jawara* in some regions) are offered and then given to family members as blessings. Return the soil to a garden.

## Variations to expect

- Some families install a clay pot of barley only; others a metal kalash only.
- In Nepal and parts of north India the sprouts are called *jamara* and worn on Vijayadashami with tika.
- In Bengal, the ghat is set up by families who hold home worship, often from Shashthi or from Pratipada.
- Whether to keep an *akhand jyoti*, and the exact objects placed inside the kalash, vary by family.
