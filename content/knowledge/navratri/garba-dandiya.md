**Garba** and **Dandiya Raas** are the devotional circle dances of [Navratri](/navratri/), rooted in Gujarat. Garba is danced with claps and turns in concentric circles around a **garbo**, a pierced clay pot with a lamp inside that symbolises life and the Goddess. Dandiya Raas is danced with a pair of decorated wooden sticks, usually with a partner, and is associated with Krishna's *raas*. Every Navratri night, from village squares in Gujarat to stadiums in Mumbai and community halls abroad, people dance to honour the Goddess. In 2023, **Garba of Gujarat** was inscribed on UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

## Origins

Garba grew out of the worship of the Goddess, especially Amba (Ambaji), in Gujarat, where women traditionally danced around the lamp in the evenings of Navratri, singing songs in praise of the Goddess. The songs themselves are called *garba* too, and many are centuries-old devotional compositions passed down orally.

Dandiya Raas comes from the *raas* tradition associated with Krishna and the gopis of Vrindavan, and with the folk stick dances of Gujarat and Rajasthan. In some tellings, the sticks represent the swords of the Goddess in her battle with Mahishasura. Over time, the two dances have come to share the same Navratri nights: often garba first, then dandiya later in the evening.

## The garbo: lamp in a pot

The word *garba* comes from Sanskrit *garbha*, "womb". The **garbo** is an earthen pot pierced with many holes, with a lamp (*garbha deep*) burning inside. The light shining through the holes is read as life within the womb, as the divine spark in every person and as the Goddess herself. The pot is placed at the centre of the circle, sometimes beside an image of the Goddess, and the dancers move around it. In some homes, women carry a lit garbo on their heads through the neighbourhood on the first night.

## What a garba night looks like

The evening usually begins with an **aarti** to the Goddess. Then the music starts, slowly at first. Dancers form circles, often several concentric rings, and move together around the centre. The tempo builds through the night. Traditional instruments are the *dhol* (drum), *shehnai* and *manjira* (small cymbals); today many events use live bands, singers and sound systems.

## Basic steps for beginners

Every community has its own variations, and there are dozens of named steps. Three common ones to start with:

1. **Be taali (two-clap):** step forward with the right foot and clap, step with the left and clap, then take a few steps turning or moving with the circle. Repeat.
2. **Tran taali (three-clap):** like the two-clap but with three claps in the cycle, often with a small turn.
3. **Dodhiyu:** a faster, bouncier step with a sway and side-to-side movement, popular as the music speeds up.

For **dandiya**, hold a stick in each hand. Partners face each other in two lines or circles, strike their sticks together and against their partner's to the beat, then move on to the next partner as the lines rotate.

Watch a round first, join an outer circle, and match the people next to you. Nobody expects perfection.

## What to wear

| | Traditional | Practical tips |
|---|---|---|
| Women | **Chaniya choli**: a flared, often mirror-worked skirt (*chaniya*) with a fitted blouse (*choli*) and an *odhni* (stole); oxidised silver jewellery | Pin the odhni securely; choose a skirt you can spin in without tripping |
| Men | **Kediyu**: a short, gathered, flared top, worn with a *dhoti* or *churidar*; sometimes a turban (*pagdi*) or a mirror-work jacket | Breathable cotton keeps you comfortable for hours |
| Feet | *Mojdi* or *jutti*, or barefoot on some grounds | Soft, closed shoes with grip protect toes in a crowded circle |

Some people follow day-specific colours during Navratri. These lists change each year with the calendar, so check a current source if you want to join in.

## Etiquette

- **Respect the centre.** The lamp and image of the Goddess are sacred; don't step into the centre or put bags and drinks near them.
- **Join from the outside.** Enter a circle gently at a gap and match its direction and speed; don't cut across.
- **Mind the sticks.** In dandiya, keep strikes controlled and at the agreed height, especially near children.
- **Follow venue rules.** Many organised events have dress codes, entry passes and no-alcohol rules.
- **Ask before photographing** people, and keep the circle free of phones and selfie sticks.
- **Stay hydrated**, and remember many dancers are fasting: offer water, not just snacks.
- **Remember it is worship.** Garba is joyful and social, but it begins and ends with the Goddess.

## Garba and Durga Puja

Bengal's [Durga Puja](/durga-puja/what-is-durga-puja/) has its own traditions of *dhunuchi naach* (dancing with incense burners) and dhak drumming rather than garba. In Kolkata, Gujarati and other communities hold garba and dandiya nights during the festival, so visitors can often enjoy a pandal visit and a garba circle in the same evening.
