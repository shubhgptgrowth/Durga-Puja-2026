**Bhai Phonta** (also written *Bhai Phota*) is the Bengali celebration of the bond between brothers and sisters, the same festival known elsewhere as **Bhai Dooj** or **Bhratri Dwitiya**. It falls on **Dwitiya**, the second lunar day of Kartik's bright fortnight, two days after [Kali Puja](/festivals/kali-puja-diwali/). Sisters put a **phonta**, a forehead mark, on their brothers, pray for their long life, and then feed them lavishly. The date changes every year with the lunar calendar, so check your local panjika.

It is one of the most loved family days of the Bengali year, and for many families it is the reason relatives travel home again after the Pujas.

## Why "phonta"?

*Phonta* (ফোঁটা) means a drop or dot, here the small mark a sister puts on her brother's forehead. In Bengal the mark is traditionally made with three things:

- **sandal paste** (*chandan*), cooling and fragrant;
- **kajal**, lamp black, often made at home over a lamp on the morning of the festival;
- **curd** (*doi*), auspicious and associated with prosperity.

Families differ on the exact recipe and order. Some add ghee, and some use only sandal paste.

## The ritual, step by step

Customs vary from home to home, but a typical Bhai Phonta looks like this:

1. **Fasting.** In many families the sister does not eat until she has given the phonta.
2. **The plate.** She prepares a plate with a lamp, sandal paste, kajal, curd, paddy and durba grass, sweets, and sometimes a conch shell.
3. **Seating.** The brother sits on a small mat or low stool (*piri*), often facing east.
4. **The phonta.** The sister puts the mark on his forehead, in many families three times and often with the little finger of her left hand, reciting the Bhai Phonta rhyme.
5. **Blessing.** She places paddy and durba grass on his head as a blessing. If she is older, he touches her feet; if he is older, she touches his.
6. **Sound.** Women in the family blow the conch and ululate (*ulu*).
7. **Sweets and gifts.** The sister offers sweets; the brother gives a gift in return, and gifts are often exchanged both ways.

Cousins and close family friends often take part, so a single sister may give phonta to a whole row of brothers.

## The chant

The best-known part of the rhyme recited while giving the phonta is a couplet almost every Bengali knows:

> ভাইয়ের কপালে দিলাম ফোঁটা, যমের দুয়ারে পড়ল কাঁটা।
> Bhaiyer kopale dilam phonta, Jomer duare porlo kanta.
> I put the mark on my brother's forehead; thorns fall at the door of Yama.

The meaning is protective: the sister's blessing places "thorns" at the door of Yama, the god of death, so that her brother cannot be taken. The lines that follow, which usually name Yamuna and Yama and ask that the brother be immortal like them, vary between families and regions, and many families have their own version passed down from grandmothers.

## The story of Yama and Yamuna

The traditional story behind the day concerns **Yama**, the god of death, and his sister **Yamuna**, the river goddess. Yamuna had long invited her brother to visit her, and he finally came on the second day of Kartik's bright fortnight. She welcomed him with great affection, put a mark on his forehead, garlanded him and served him a feast. Moved by her care, Yama granted her a boon: that any brother who receives a mark and a meal from his sister on this day will be protected from untimely death.

Versions differ in detail, and in other parts of India the festival is also linked with Krishna visiting his sister Subhadra after defeating the demon Narakasura. The idea at the heart of each version is the same: a sister's love as a shield for her brother.

## The meal

After the ritual comes the real event for many: a big lunch cooked by the sister or the household. There is no fixed menu, but common dishes include:

- rice with ghee, shukto, dal and fried vegetables;
- fish, often rui or ilish, or mutton or chicken in families that eat them;
- chutney, papad, payesh and plenty of mishti (sweets) such as sandesh and rosogolla.

Sweet shops in Kolkata are especially busy on Bhai Phonta morning, and a special box of sweets is part of most visits. For more on Bengali festive cooking, see [food and bhog](/durga-puja/food-and-bhog/).

## Gifts

Brothers traditionally give their sisters clothes, jewellery or money, and sisters often give gifts in return, such as a shirt or kurta. Today gift-giving goes both ways, and for brothers and sisters living far apart, phonta is sometimes given over a video call, with a mark placed on a photo or on the wall.

## Where it fits in the season

Bhai Phonta closes the cluster of festivals around the Kartik new moon: Bhoot Chaturdashi, then Kali Puja and Diwali, then Bhai Phonta. Later in the same fortnight comes [Jagaddhatri Puja](/festivals/jagaddhatri-puja-chandannagar/). See the [festival season hub](/festivals/) for the full sequence.
