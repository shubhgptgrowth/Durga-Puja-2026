**Kali Puja**, also called **Shyama Puja**, is the worship of the goddess Kali on the **new moon night (Amavasya) of Kartik**. In Bengal it is the great festival of the Diwali night: while much of India worships Lakshmi and lights lamps for Diwali, Bengal also lights lamps but worships Kali, usually through the dark hours. It comes **about three weeks after Bijoya Dashami** (in 2026 Dashami is on 21 October); for the exact date and the timing of the Amavasya tithi, check your local panjika.

After the gentle household Lakshmi Puja on the full moon, Kali Puja brings the season back into the streets, with pandals, drums, lights and crowds.

## Who is Kali?

Kali is one of the most powerful forms of the Great Goddess. In the Devi Mahatmya she emerges from Durga's brow to destroy the demons Chanda and Munda and helps defeat Raktabija, whose every drop of spilt blood became a new demon, by drinking his blood before it touches the ground. She is also the first of the ten [Dashamahavidya](/durga-puja/dashamahavidya/), the wisdom goddesses of Shakta tradition. In Bengal she is loved as a mother as much as she is feared: the poet-devotee Ramprasad Sen's songs to "Ma" are still sung at her worship.

## How Kali is shown

The Bengali image of Kali, most often in the form called **Dakshina Kali**, is easy to recognise:

- dark blue or black skin, with loose, flowing hair;
- four arms: one holding a sword, one a severed head, and two making gestures of protection and blessing;
- a garland of heads and a skirt of severed arms;
- a red tongue, often shown out, between her teeth;
- standing on the reclining body of Shiva.

The images can look fierce to newcomers. Devotees read them symbolically: the severed heads as the ego cut away, the blessing hands as her care for those who come to her, and Shiva beneath her as the stillness that holds her energy. A popular story says she stepped on Shiva in battle frenzy and bit her tongue in recognition, though interpretations differ.

## A night of worship

Kali Puja is usually performed at night, often around the middle of the night, and in many places it continues until dawn. Typical elements include:

- a lamp, incense and a large offering of red hibiscus (*joba*), Kali's favourite flower;
- recitation of mantras and the names of the goddess, with conch and bells;
- bhog, which in many homes and temples is vegetarian (khichuri, fried items, sweets), while some traditions offer other foods according to their custom;
- *homa* (fire offering) towards the end, and devotional songs through the night.

Practice varies widely between Vaishnava-influenced households, Shakta and Tantric lineages, temples and community committees. Some older traditions include rites not followed in most homes today. If you attend, follow the lead of the family or temple you are visiting.

## Dakshineswar and Kalighat

Two Kolkata temples are especially associated with Kali:

- **Dakshineswar Kali Temple**, on the east bank of the Hooghly north of the city, was founded in the mid-nineteenth century by Rani Rashmoni. It is famous as the place where Sri Ramakrishna served as priest. On Kali Puja night it draws very large crowds.
- **Kalighat Kali Temple**, in south Kolkata, is one of the city's oldest and best-known shrines and is counted among the Shakti Peethas, the sacred seats of the goddess. Its striking image of Kali, with a long golden tongue, is unlike the classic pandal idol.

Expect long queues at both on Kali Puja and the days around it. Go early, carry little, and follow temple and police instructions.

## Bhoot Chaturdashi: the night before

The evening before Kali Puja is **Bhoot Chaturdashi**, the fourteenth lunar day of the dark fortnight. Two customs mark it:

- **Choddo prodip**: fourteen small clay lamps are lit around the house, at doorways, windows and dark corners, to keep away harmful spirits and to light the way for ancestors.
- **Choddo shaak**: fourteen kinds of leafy greens are cooked together and eaten, a custom explained as both honouring ancestors and protecting health at the turn of the season. Which fourteen greens are used varies by family and market.

## Lamps, lights and fireworks

On Kali Puja night, Bengali homes are lit with clay lamps (*diya* or *prodip*) and candles, and increasingly with electric fairy lights. Fireworks are a traditional part of the night for many families. In recent years courts and pollution control authorities have placed limits on which firecrackers may be sold and burst, and at what hours, because of air and noise pollution. Rules change from year to year, so check current local rules before buying or using any. Keep water nearby, supervise children, keep pets indoors and remember neighbours who are elderly or unwell.

## Pandals and community pujas

Many neighbourhoods across Kolkata and Bengal hold community Kali Pujas with pandals and large idols, and some towns are known regionally for their Kali Puja celebrations. The atmosphere is livelier and later than Durga Puja, with crowds out well after midnight.

## What follows

Two days after Kali Puja comes [Bhai Phonta](/festivals/bhai-phonta/), when sisters bless their brothers, and later in the fortnight [Jagaddhatri Puja](/festivals/jagaddhatri-puja-chandannagar/).
