**Vishwakarma Puja** (in Bengali often *Biswakarma Puja*) is the worship of **Vishwakarma**, the divine architect, engineer and craftsman of Hindu tradition. It **usually falls on 17 September**, because it follows the solar calendar: it is held on **Kanya Sankranti**, when the sun enters the sign of Virgo, the last day of the Bengali month of Bhadra. In Bengal it is a festival of work and workplaces. Factories, workshops, garages, printing presses, shops and offices clean and decorate their tools and machines and worship Vishwakarma, and in Kolkata the sky fills with kites.

For many Bengalis the day has another meaning too: it is the first festival of the autumn season and a sure sign that [Durga Puja](/durga-puja/) is only weeks away.

## Who is Vishwakarma?

In Hindu tradition Vishwakarma is the craftsman of the gods. Myths and epics credit him with building palaces and cities for the gods, and with forging divine weapons and chariots. Accounts vary from text to text, and in some traditions he is also seen as a creator of the universe itself, which is one meaning of his name, "maker of all".

He is the patron deity of anyone who makes or builds: carpenters, smiths, masons, weavers, mechanics, engineers, architects and factory workers. Several artisan communities in India regard him as their ancestor.

### How he is shown in Bengal

Bengali images of Vishwakarma have a recognisable style. He is usually shown as a young or middle-aged man with a moustache, with four arms holding tools such as a hammer, a measuring instrument or a scale, and with an **elephant** as his vahana (mount). Elsewhere in India he is often depicted as an older, bearded sage riding a swan. Both forms are traditional.

## Why the date is fixed

Most Bengali festivals follow the lunar calendar and move around the Gregorian calendar from year to year. Vishwakarma Puja is different: it is tied to a **sankranti**, the sun's passage from one zodiac sign to the next, and solar dates stay close to the Gregorian ones. That is why it usually falls on 17 September, occasionally a day either side, depending on the exact time of the sankranti. If you need certainty for a particular year, check the panjika.

## Worship in factories and workshops

Vishwakarma Puja is, above all, a workplace festival. On the day:

- **Machines rest.** Tools and machinery are cleaned, oiled, decorated with flowers, sandal paste and vermilion marks, and not used for work.
- **The image is installed.** A clay image of Vishwakarma is set up on the shop floor, in the workshop or in a small pandal outside the factory gate.
- **The puja.** A priest performs the worship, often in the morning, with flowers, fruit, sweets and incense; workers and owners attend together.
- **Prasad and a meal.** Prasad is shared, and many workplaces hold a meal for all staff, followed by music or a small celebration.

Vehicles get their share: taxis, autorickshaws, buses, lorries and private cars are washed and garlanded. Computer and electronics shops, printers, and even offices with nothing more than desks and laptops often hold a small puja. For many workers it is one of the few days in the year when the whole workforce celebrates together.

The day also shows Bengal's industrial history. Along the Hooghly, where jute mills, engineering works and dockyards grew up, Vishwakarma Puja has long been a major event, and in some industrial areas it rivals the bigger festivals in local importance.

## Kite flying in Kolkata

The most visible custom in Kolkata is **kite flying** (*ghuri orano*). From morning, rooftops in many neighbourhoods are crowded with families and friends flying paper kites, and the sky fills with colour. Kite fights, in which one flier tries to cut another's string, are part of the fun, and the cry of "*bho-kaatta!*" goes up whenever a kite is cut loose. Kite shops in the old city are busy in the days before.

There is no single agreed explanation of why kites are flown on this day; it is best described as a cherished local custom. A few safety points are worth keeping in mind:

- avoid flying kites near overhead electric lines and railway tracks;
- do not use glass-coated or synthetic string, which injures people and birds and is restricted in many places;
- keep children away from unprotected roof edges.
