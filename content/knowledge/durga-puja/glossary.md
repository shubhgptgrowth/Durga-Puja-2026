## How Bengali puja vocabulary works

Durga Puja words come from two layers of language. The ritual words are mostly **Sanskrit**: the names of rites such as *bodhon* (awakening), *prana pratishtha* (installing life) and *pushpanjali* (flower offering), which the priest uses because the mantras themselves are in Sanskrit. The everyday words are **Bengali**: *pujo*, *thakur*, *dhak*, *bhog*, *kolakuli*. In practice people mix both, so a single conversation can move from *sandhi puja* to *thakur dekha* without a pause.

### Pujo or puja?

*Puja* is the standard word for worship across India. Bengali speakers usually say **pujo** (পুজো), because spoken Bengali tends to round the final "a" sound. Written Bengali also has the more formal *puja* (পূজা). On its own, "Pujo" almost always means Durga Puja, as in "Where are you going this Pujo?"

### The "o" sound

The same pattern explains many spellings. Bengali pronounces the short "a" of Sanskrit closer to an "o", so *Bodhan* becomes *Bodhon*, *Mangal* is said "mongol", and *Saptami* is often said "Shoptomi". You will see words spelled both ways in English; neither is wrong. Similarly, Bengali usually pronounces "s" as "sh", which is why *sindoor* is often said "shindur" and *Shashthi* "Shoshthi".

### Thakur is the deity

In everyday Bengali, **thakur** means a deity, and at Pujo it refers to the image of Durga and her family. When people say *thakur dekhte jachchhi* ("I'm going to see the thakur"), they mean pandal hopping. The same word can also be a surname or a term of respect, so the meaning comes from context.

### Ma, Uma and Durga

Bengalis speak of the goddess as **Ma** (mother) and also as **Uma**, the daughter who visits her parents' home for a few days each autumn before returning to Kailash. Much of the emotion of the festival, from the welcome on Shashthi to the tears on Dashami, comes from this homecoming story, alongside the warrior goddess who defeats Mahishasura.

### Days are tithis

Puja days are named after **tithis**, lunar days: *Shashthi* (sixth), *Saptami* (seventh), *Ashtami* (eighth), *Navami* (ninth) and *Dashami* (tenth). Because tithis do not line up neatly with calendar days, timings for rituals such as *sandhi puja* change every year and can differ slightly between almanacs. For exact timings, check your local panjika. The 2026 puja dates are on our [dates page](/guide/dates/).

### Sounds have names too

Many puja words describe sounds: the *dhak* drum, the *kashor* gong, the *shankh* (conch) and *ulu*, the ululation women make at auspicious moments. Together they are what people mean when they talk about "the sound of Pujo". Read more on the [dhak and dhaki](/durga-puja/dhak-and-dhaki/) page.
