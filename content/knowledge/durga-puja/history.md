Durga Puja in Bengal has moved, over roughly three centuries, from the courtyards of a few wealthy houses to the streets of an entire city. The shape of that journey is clear even where individual dates are debated: first household pujas of landowners and kings, then pujas paid for by groups of neighbours (*barowari*), then fully public pujas open to everyone (*sarbojanin*), and finally the theme pandals and global recognition of today. Much of the early history survives as family tradition and later retelling, so the dates below are given as they are traditionally cited.

## Before Kolkata: rajas and zamindars

The worship of Durga in Bengal is far older than the big autumn festival. Bengali texts on the goddess and her worship were in circulation by the late medieval period, and the autumn puja with a clay image of the goddess and her family took shape among the region's landholding elite.

Many accounts point to the late sixteenth century, when a landowner, Raja Kangsanarayan of Taherpur, is said to have held a lavish Durga Puja; this is commonly cited as one of the earliest grand pujas in Bengal, though it rests on tradition rather than contemporary records. In the eighteenth century **Raja Krishnachandra Roy of Nadia** (Krishnanagar), a celebrated patron of religion and the arts, is credited with popularising elaborate Durga and Jagaddhatri pujas. For a *zamindar* (landholder) or raja, the puja was devotion, but also a display of wealth and standing: feasts for the poor, music and dance, and invitations to the powerful.

In the Kolkata area, the **Sabarna Roy Choudhury** family's puja at Barisha is often said to date to the early seventeenth century, which would make it the oldest in the region. Like most such claims, the date comes from family tradition.

## Sovabazar Rajbari and the colonial city

The most famous early puja in Kolkata is at **[Sovabazar Rajbari](/guide/pandals/sovabazar_rajbari/)** in the north of the city, the palace of **Raja Nabakrishna Deb**. His Durga Puja is traditionally dated to **1757**, the year of the Battle of Plassey, and is widely said to have been held partly to celebrate the British victory, with **Robert Clive** among the guests. Nabakrishna had worked closely with the East India Company and rose with it.

Whether or not every detail is exact, the story captures something real about eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Kolkata. The new merchant and landed families of the colonial capital, the *babus*, competed in the scale of their pujas, inviting Company officials, hiring famous singers and dancers, and spending lavishly. These households, now called *bonedi bari* (old aristocratic houses), still hold many of the city's most traditional pujas. Read more in [Bonedi bari pujas](/durga-puja/bonedi-bari-pujas/).

## Barowari: the puja of the twelve friends

The first step away from the single patron was the **barowari** puja, funded by subscription. The usual origin story places it at **Guptipara** in Hooghly district, where, it is said, twelve friends who were not allowed to take part in a household puja pooled money to hold their own. The date commonly given is **around 1790**, and the word is popularly explained as *baro* (twelve) and *yaar* (friends). Historians treat both the date and the etymology with some caution, but the idea it describes, a puja owned by a group instead of a family, changed everything that followed.

Through the nineteenth century, barowari pujas spread across Bengal's towns. Subscriptions, not one family's purse, now paid for the image, the priest and the celebrations.

## Sarbojanin: a puja for everyone

In the early twentieth century, Kolkata saw the rise of the **sarbojanin** (for everyone) puja, organised by a neighbourhood committee and open to all regardless of caste, class or means. A puja in Bhowanipore in 1910 is frequently cited as the first sarbojanin puja in the city, and many famous committees trace their origins to the 1910s and 1920s.

Among them is **[Bagbazar Sarbojanin](/guide/pandals/bagbazar/)** in north Kolkata, whose beginnings are usually dated to 1918 or 1919 (accounts differ) and which is still known for a traditional image and a large fair. Nearby, the potters' quarter of **Kumartuli** supplied images for the growing number of pujas; see [Kumartuli and idol making](/durga-puja/kumartuli-idol-making/) and visit [Kumartuli Park](/guide/pandals/kumartuli_park/).

## The nationalist era

The sarbojanin puja grew up at the same time as the freedom movement, and the two became linked. Community pujas were gathering places, and in the 1920s and 1930s some were associated with nationalist leaders, most famously **Subhas Chandra Bose**, who was connected with several Kolkata puja committees. Pujas encouraged *swadeshi* (Indian-made) goods, physical culture clubs organised pujas, and the image of the goddess as mother was easily read as an image of the motherland. The colonial authorities sometimes viewed these gatherings with suspicion.

## After independence: the neighbourhood festival

From the mid-twentieth century Kolkata's population grew rapidly, including many families who came from East Bengal after Partition, and new neighbourhoods built their own pujas. The city's festival became a dense network of local committees, each raising funds from residents and businesses. Images grew larger, lighting more ambitious, and pandals began to copy famous buildings. Light artists from Chandannagar became known for illuminated displays.

## The theme-pandal era

From the 1980s and especially through the late 1990s and 2000s, Kolkata's pujas turned into a large public art event. **Theme pandals** replaced the standard decorated structure with a single artistic idea: a village craft, a social issue, a material such as clay pots or jute, or an imagined world. The image itself was often redesigned to match. Trained artists and art-college graduates were commissioned, corporate sponsors offered awards for the best pandals, and crowds began to travel across the city to see the most talked-about ones.

Today the big community pujas of central and south Kolkata are as much about art and design as worship, while the bonedi bari and older sarbojanin pujas keep traditional forms. Both are part of the festival. Our [central Kolkata blockbusters trail](/guide/trails/central_blockbusters/) and [south Kolkata classics trail](/guide/trails/south_classic/) show the range.

## UNESCO recognition, 2021

In December 2021 **Durga Puja in Kolkata** was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The listing recognises the festival as a living tradition of craft, performance and community that cuts across religion, caste and class. See [Durga Puja and UNESCO](/durga-puja/unesco-heritage/).

## Seeing the history today

North Kolkata is the best place to feel the older layers of this story. A single walk can take in a rajbari puja, an early sarbojanin puja and the workshops where images are still made by hand. Follow the [North Kolkata heritage trail](/guide/trails/north_heritage/), which links [Sovabazar Rajbari](/guide/pandals/sovabazar_rajbari/), [Bagbazar](/guide/pandals/bagbazar/) and [Kumartuli Park](/guide/pandals/kumartuli_park/), and see the [north Kolkata area guide](/guide/areas/north/). In 2026 the main puja days run from Shashthi on Saturday 17 October to Bijoya Dashami on Wednesday 21 October; family pujas often have limited visiting hours, so check locally before you go.
