The short answer: **choose clay over plaster of Paris, natural colours over toxic paint, controlled immersion over dumping in rivers, and reusable materials over plastic and thermocol.** Durga Puja's traditional materials were already close to the earth: straw, bamboo, river clay and plant-based colours. Many of today's environmental problems come from newer additions, so going greener often means going back to the old ways, while following current rules on immersion. Here's what matters, for pujas big and small.

## Clay versus plaster of Paris

The classic Bengal Durga is built on a bamboo and straw frame, coated in layers of clay, often including clay from the banks of the Ganga, then dried and painted. You can watch this process in [Kumartuli](/durga-puja/kumartuli-idol-making/). Unbaked clay softens and breaks down in water, returning to the riverbed as sediment.

**Plaster of Paris** (PoP) idols are lighter, quicker to cast and often cheaper, and they are common in some festivals elsewhere in India. But PoP sets hard and dissolves very slowly in water, so immersed idols can sit in rivers, lakes and ponds for a long time. Pollution regulators in India have discouraged or restricted PoP idols in many places. Bengal's Durga idols are still largely clay-based, but ornaments, bases and decorations can include PoP, fibreglass or thermocol.

What to look for:

- An idol made of **straw, bamboo and unbaked clay**.
- **No thermocol** or fibreglass ornaments, or ornaments that can be removed and kept before immersion.
- A **smaller idol**: less clay, less paint, easier immersion.

## Natural and non-toxic colours

Idols are traditionally painted with mineral and plant-based pigments and a natural binder. Bright synthetic paints, some of which have contained heavy metals such as lead or chromium, came later. When colours wash off into water during immersion, those chemicals go with them.

- Ask the artisan whether they use water-based, non-toxic or natural colours.
- Choose lightly painted or unpainted idols for home pujas.
- Avoid glitter and metallic sprays, which are usually plastic-based or synthetic.

## Immersion: rivers, rules and artificial ponds

*Bisarjan* (immersion) on [Bijoya Dashami](/durga-puja/rituals/dashami/), which falls on Wednesday 21 October in 2026, is the most moving moment of the festival, and also the one with the biggest environmental footprint. A great many idols, with their flowers, cloth, ornaments and frames, go into the Hooghly and local ponds over a few days.

Over the years, **pollution control boards, courts and local authorities** in India have issued guidelines on idol materials, colours and immersion. They have covered matters such as discouraging PoP and toxic paint, removing flowers, cloth and ornaments before immersion, collecting debris quickly, and providing designated immersion sites. The details vary by state and city and change from year to year, so we don't summarise specific rulings here. **Check current notices** from the West Bengal Pollution Control Board, your municipality and the police before you plan an immersion.

In Kolkata, the municipal authorities have typically arranged designated ghats, cranes and clearing crews so that frames and debris are removed soon after immersion. In many cities and housing complexes, **artificial ponds or immersion tanks** are set up so that idols never enter a natural water body; the clay settles and is collected separately. If one is available near you, it is the simplest green choice.

Before immersion, families and committees can:

- Remove flowers, garlands, cloth, ornaments and plastic for composting, reuse or proper disposal.
- Keep ritual items such as the *kola bou* materials and leftover samagri for composting where customs allow.
- Avoid throwing plastic bags, bottles and decorations into the water.

## Reducing plastic and waste

A large pandal generates a lot of waste over five days: plates, cups, bottles, flex banners, thermocol and food. Committees and visitors both have a part to play.

**For committees:**

- Use bamboo, cloth, jute and other reusable or biodegradable materials in the pandal; reuse frames and panels. See [pandal art and themes](/durga-puja/pandal-art-and-themes/) for how pandals are built.
- Serve bhog on sal-leaf, banana-leaf or areca plates, or in steel, rather than thermocol or plastic.
- Provide clearly marked bins for wet and dry waste, and arrange collection with local authorities.
- Use LED lighting, and switch off decorative lights in the small hours if possible.
- Replace plastic flex with cloth or reusable signs.

**For visitors:**

- Carry a refillable water bottle and a cloth bag.
- Eat from stalls that use leaf or paper plates, and put waste in bins.
- Skip plastic toys, balloons and glitter.

## Keeping the noise down

The [dhak](/durga-puja/dhak-and-dhaki/) is the soul of pujo, but loudspeakers and firecrackers can push noise far beyond comfort, especially for elderly people, infants, patients and animals. India has rules on noise limits and loudspeaker hours, and West Bengal has had its own restrictions on firecrackers; these are set and enforced locally and change from year to year, so check current notices. Committees can keep amplified sound moderate and switch it off at night, and families can skip loud firecrackers altogether.

## A greener puja at home

If you hold a small [puja at home](/durga-puja/at-home/), you have the most control:

- **Idol.** A small, unpainted or naturally coloured clay idol, or a picture or *ghot* (sacred pot) instead, as many families have traditionally done.
- **Decoration.** Fresh flowers, mango leaves, banana stems, alpana drawn with rice paste, and cloth instead of plastic.
- **Lighting.** Clay diyas and LED strings instead of disposable plastic decorations.
- **Bhog and prasad.** Serve on steel or leaf plates; see our [samagri list](/durga-puja/samagri-list/) for natural materials.
- **Flowers afterwards.** Compost them or put them under a tree, rather than in a plastic bag in the river.
- **Immersion at home.** Many families now immerse a small clay idol in a bucket or tub of water, let it dissolve, and pour the clay into a garden or potted plant. Ask your family priest whether this fits your tradition.

## Is an eco-friendly puja still traditional?

Yes. Clay, straw, natural colours, flowers, leaf plates and the dhak are the oldest parts of the festival. Many puja committees in Kolkata have already built eco-themed pandals, and some, such as [Mudiali Club](/guide/pandals/mudiali/), have been associated with environmental themes. Rituals vary by family and panjika, so speak with your priest about any change, but the spirit of the festival, welcoming the goddess home and letting her go with love, does not depend on plastic.
