Durga Puja is the worship of the goddess Durga, the fierce and protective form of the divine mother, held each autumn. At its heart is a story: the goddess, created from the combined power of all the gods, defeats the buffalo demon Mahishasura and saves the world. In Bengal that cosmic story sits alongside a very human one. The same goddess is Uma, a married daughter who comes home to her parents for a few days a year, bringing her children, and whose departure on the tenth day is mourned like any family farewell. Understanding both stories explains almost everything you will see at a puja.

## The Devi Mahatmya: the source of the story

The main scriptural source is the **Devi Mahatmya** (*Glory of the Goddess*), a Sanskrit text of about 700 verses that forms part of the Markandeya Purana. It is also known as the *Durga Saptashati* (the seven hundred verses) or, in Bengal, simply as the *Chandi*. Scholars usually place its composition in the middle of the first millennium CE. Its recitation, *Chandi path*, is part of the worship during Durga Puja, and its verses are the backbone of the famous dawn broadcast on [Mahalaya](/durga-puja/rituals/mahalaya/), the [Mahishasuramardini](/durga-puja/mahishasuramardini-radio/) programme.

The text tells three episodes of the goddess fighting demons. The middle one is the best known.

## The slaying of Mahishasura

Mahishasura, a demon who could take the form of a buffalo, grows so powerful that he drives the gods out of heaven. The gods go to Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva, and their anger blazes out as light. That radiance, joined with the light of every other god, takes shape as a single goddess of overwhelming beauty and strength.

Each god then gives her a weapon or gift: commonly Shiva's trident, Vishnu's discus, Indra's thunderbolt, and a lion to ride, given by the Himalaya. She enters battle, destroys Mahishasura's army and finally kills him as he emerges from the body of the buffalo. This is the moment frozen in the Bengali clay image, which is why Durga is often called *Mahishasuramardini*, slayer of Mahishasura. Our guide to [the goddess and her iconography](/durga-puja/goddess-durga/) explains the image in detail.

## Durga as Shakti

In Hindu thought, *Shakti* is the active power of the divine, often personified as the goddess. Durga is one of her greatest forms. Her name is commonly explained as "the one who is difficult to approach" or "the remover of difficulties", and she is at once warrior and mother. Devotees ask her for protection, strength and the removal of obstacles, and the emphasis on the goddess as the supreme power makes Durga Puja central to the *Shakta* tradition, which is strong in Bengal and Assam.

## Uma comes home

Bengali religious culture adds a tender layer. Durga is identified with **Uma** (Parvati), daughter of Giriraj Himalaya and his wife Menaka, who married Shiva and lives far away on Mount Kailash. Each autumn she is allowed to visit her parents' home for a few days with her four children: [Lakshmi, Saraswati, Ganesha and Kartik](/durga-puja/goddess-durga/).

This is why Bengali pujas show the whole family on one platform, and why the festival feels like a homecoming. Traditional *agomani* songs express Menaka's longing for her daughter's arrival, and *bijoya* songs express the grief of her leaving. On Dashami, married women bid her farewell as they would a daughter going back to her in-laws, with sweets, vermilion and tears; see [sindoor khela](/durga-puja/rituals/sindoor-khela/) and [Bijoya Dashami](/durga-puja/rituals/dashami/).

## Akal Bodhon: the untimely awakening

In older practice the goddess was worshipped in spring, in the month of Chaitra, as **Basanti Puja**, which some families still hold. Why, then, is the great puja in autumn?

Bengali tradition answers with the **akal bodhon**, the untimely (*akal*) awakening (*bodhon*). It is told in the Ramayana of Krittibas Ojha, the much-loved Bengali retelling of the epic, and not in Valmiki's Sanskrit Ramayana. Before his battle with Ravana, Rama invokes the goddess out of season to win her blessing. He gathers 108 blue lotuses for the worship, and when the goddess hides one to test him, he prepares to offer one of his own eyes, often described as lotus-like, in its place. Moved by his devotion, she stops him and grants him victory.

The idea of an "untimely" puja also connects to the belief that the gods sleep during the southern half of the sun's year, so the goddess must be ritually woken. That waking is performed on Shashthi evening at the *bilva* (bel) tree, a rite still called bodhon; see [Shashthi](/durga-puja/rituals/shashthi/).

## Why autumn: Sharadiya Durgotsav

The festival is formally the **Sharadiya** (autumn) Durga Puja, held in the bright fortnight of Ashwin, which usually falls in late September or October. In Bengal this is the end of the monsoon: clear skies, white *kash* grass flowering on riverbanks, and *shiuli* (night-flowering jasmine) on the ground in the mornings. These images appear in songs and poems as signs that the goddess is on her way.

The same lunar fortnight is kept across much of India as [Navratri](/navratri/), the nine nights of the goddess, ending in Dussehra or [Vijayadashami](/navratri/dussehra-vijayadashami/).

## What the days feel like

The season opens on [Mahalaya](/durga-puja/rituals/mahalaya/), the new moon that ends the fortnight of remembering ancestors and begins the *Devi Paksha*, the fortnight of the goddess. In 2026 Mahalaya is on Saturday 10 October. Many households wake before dawn to the radio.

The main puja then runs from the sixth lunar day to the tenth:

- **Shashthi** (Saturday 17 October 2026): the goddess is awakened and her face is unveiled. See [Shashthi](/durga-puja/rituals/shashthi/).
- **Saptami** (Sunday 18 October): the [Kola Bou](/durga-puja/rituals/kola-bou/), a banana plant dressed as a bride, is bathed at dawn, and the life of the goddess is invoked into the image. See [Saptami](/durga-puja/rituals/saptami/).
- **Maha Ashtami** (Monday 19 October): the most crowded morning, with [pushpanjali](/durga-puja/rituals/pushpanjali-mantra/), [Kumari Puja](/durga-puja/rituals/kumari-puja/) and, at the junction of Ashtami and Navami, [Sandhi Puja](/durga-puja/rituals/sandhi-puja/). See [Ashtami](/durga-puja/rituals/ashtami/).
- **Maha Navami** (Tuesday 20 October): the last full day, with a sense that time is running out. See [Navami](/durga-puja/rituals/navami/).
- **Bijoya Dashami** (Wednesday 21 October): farewell, immersion and the visits and sweets of *Bijoya*. See [Dashami](/durga-puja/rituals/dashami/).

Outside the rituals, the days are about people: new clothes, the beat of the [dhak](/durga-puja/dhak-and-dhaki/), the [bhog](/durga-puja/food-and-bhog/) shared at community pujas, evening [dhunuchi dance](/durga-puja/rituals/dhunuchi-naach/), and long nights walking from one pandal to the next. Exact ritual timings depend on the almanac followed, so check your local *panjika* or the puja committee's notice.
