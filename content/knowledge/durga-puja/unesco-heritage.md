In December 2021, UNESCO inscribed **"Durga Puja in Kolkata"** on its **Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity**. The decision was taken at the annual session of the intergovernmental committee that oversees UNESCO's 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, on a nomination submitted by India. It placed Kolkata's festival alongside other living traditions from around the world, and it was celebrated in the city as international recognition of something Bengalis had long regarded as their greatest public occasion.

## What "intangible cultural heritage" means

UNESCO runs two quite different heritage programmes, and they are often confused.

- **World Heritage** (the 1972 Convention) covers places: monuments, buildings and natural sites such as the Sundarbans.
- **Intangible Cultural Heritage** (the 2003 Convention) covers living practices: oral traditions, performing arts, rituals and festivals, knowledge and craft skills that communities pass from one generation to the next.

Durga Puja belongs to the second. What is recognised is not a pandal or an image, which are made each year and then dismantled or immersed, but the practice itself: the knowledge of the image-makers, the rituals, the music and the way neighbourhoods organise and share the festival.

The **Representative List** is meant to raise awareness of the diversity of living heritage. Unlike the separate list of heritage in need of urgent safeguarding, it does not imply the tradition is in danger.

## Why Durga Puja in Kolkata

UNESCO's description of the element, on its [official page](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/durga-puja-in-kolkata-01503), presents Durga Puja as a festival that combines religious worship with public art and performance on a huge scale. Read alongside the nomination, several reasons stand out.

### A community-made festival

Kolkata's pujas are overwhelmingly organised by neighbourhood committees, the *sarbojanin* (for everyone) pujas described in our [history of Durga Puja](/durga-puja/history/). Residents raise funds, plan themes, cook, keep order and volunteer through the nights. The festival is created by the people who celebrate it, which is exactly the kind of community ownership the 2003 Convention is designed to recognise.

### A festival of art and craft

The festival sustains an entire economy of traditional and contemporary skills: the image-makers of [Kumartuli](/durga-puja/kumartuli-idol-making/), the *shola* and *daker saaj* ornament makers, pandal builders, painters, lighting artists, [dhak drummers](/durga-puja/dhak-and-dhaki/), priests, cooks and many more. Theme pandals have turned parts of the city into an open-air exhibition where established artists and young designers show work to millions of visitors.

### An inclusive public space

Although Durga Puja is a Hindu festival, its public pandals are open to anyone. People of other faiths and of none walk the streets, visit pandals, work on them and share the food and music. The festival's ability to bring together people across religion, caste, class and gender was central to how it was presented to UNESCO.

## What the listing does, and does not, do

An inscription is a form of recognition, not a law. It does not:

- give UNESCO any control over how the festival is held;
- protect a particular style of image or ritual from change;
- bring regular funding by itself.

It does:

- put the festival on an international list that raises its profile with travellers, researchers and the media;
- commit the nominating state to safeguarding measures, and to reporting periodically on how the tradition is faring;
- give communities and artisans a stronger voice when they ask for support.

Living heritage is expected to change. A theme pandal made of recycled materials is as much a part of the tradition UNESCO recognised as an ekchala image in an old family courtyard.

## What changed after 2021

The most visible change was a burst of civic pride. Kolkata marked the recognition with public celebrations, including a large procession in 2022, and the listing began to appear in tourism campaigns, on puja banners and in press coverage abroad. Interest from foreign visitors, photographers and researchers grew, and more organised heritage walks and puja tours appeared in north Kolkata.

The listing also sharpened long-running questions: how to keep the artisans of Kumartuli and the ornament and drum traditions economically viable, how to reduce the environmental impact of immersion, paint and lighting, and how to manage crowd safety as the festival keeps growing. These are not new problems, but heritage status gave them a wider audience.

## Experiencing the heritage yourself

The best way to understand what UNESCO recognised is to see the whole chain, from workshop to pandal to immersion.

1. Visit [Kumartuli](/durga-puja/kumartuli-idol-making/) in the weeks before the festival, when the images are being finished; in 2026, chokkhu daan, the painting of the eyes, is traditionally around Mahalaya on Saturday 10 October.
2. Walk the [North Kolkata heritage trail](/guide/trails/north_heritage/) to see traditional pujas such as [Bagbazar](/guide/pandals/bagbazar/) and [Kumartuli Park](/guide/pandals/kumartuli_park/).
3. Spend an evening among the theme pandals of central and south Kolkata, for example on the [central Kolkata trail](/guide/trails/central_blockbusters/) that includes [College Square](/guide/pandals/college_square/).
4. Join a morning [pushpanjali](/durga-puja/rituals/pushpanjali-mantra/) on Maha Ashtami (Monday 19 October 2026), and watch the farewell rituals of [Bijoya Dashami](/durga-puja/rituals/dashami/) on Wednesday 21 October 2026.

Our [pandal hopping tips](/durga-puja/pandal-hopping-tips/) and the [Kolkata guide](/guide/) will help you plan the route.
