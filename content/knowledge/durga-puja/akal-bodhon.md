**Akal Bodhon** is the Bengali name for the story that explains why Durga Puja is held in autumn. *Akal* means untimely or out of season; *bodhon* means awakening. Before his final battle with Ravana, Rama is said to have awakened and worshipped the goddess Durga in the autumn month of Ashwin, outside her usual season, to win her blessing. Bengali tradition sees every Sharadiya (autumn) Durga Puja as following his example. The rite of bodhon itself is still performed at the start of the puja, on [Shashthi](/durga-puja/rituals/shashthi/) evening.

## Where the story comes from

The best-known source is the **Krittivasi Ramayana**, the Bengali retelling of the Ramayana by the poet **Krittibas Ojha**, usually dated to around the fifteenth century. Written in simple Bengali verse, it became one of the most widely read books in Bengal, recited in homes and at gatherings for centuries, and it shaped how Bengalis imagine Rama's story.

It is important to be clear: **the episode is not in Valmiki's Sanskrit Ramayana.** In Valmiki, before the decisive duel, the sage Agastya teaches Rama the *Aditya Hridayam*, a hymn to the sun, and it is the sun's blessing he seeks. The idea that Rama worshipped Durga out of season appears in some later Sanskrit texts associated with eastern India, and the Kalika Purana and the Brihaddharma Purana are often cited for it, but it was Krittibas's telling that made it part of everyday Bengali belief. Details differ between versions and manuscripts; what follows is the outline most Bengalis know.

## Rama's untimely puja

The war in Lanka is going badly. In the Bengali telling Ravana is himself a devotee of the goddess and enjoys her protection, and Rama cannot defeat him. Rama is advised, in the commonly told version by Brahma, that he must win the goddess to his side by worshipping her himself.

There is a problem of timing. The goddess's proper worship falls in spring. Autumn lies in *dakshinayana*, the half of the sun's year that counts as the night of the gods, when they sleep. To worship her now, Rama must first wake her. So he performs the bodhon at the foot of a bel tree, on the sixth lunar day, and begins the puja.

## The 108 blue lotuses

For the worship Rama needs **108 blue lotuses** (*nil padma*), a rare flower. Hanuman flies off and brings them back from a distant lake. As the worship nears its climax, in many tellings at the juncture of the eighth and ninth lunar days, Rama counts the flowers and finds only 107. The goddess herself has hidden one to test his devotion.

There is no time to search again. Then Rama remembers that people call him *Rajivlochan* or *Kamallochan*, the lotus-eyed. If his eyes are like blue lotuses, one of them can complete the offering. He takes up an arrow to pluck out his eye.

Before he can do it, the goddess appears and stops his hand. Moved by his devotion, she blesses him and promises him victory. In the story Rama goes on to defeat Ravana, and the goddess is bid farewell on the tenth day: the Vijaya or Bijoya Dashami.

Bengali pujas still carry echoes of this tale. The 108 lotuses and 108 lamps offered at [Sandhi Puja](/durga-puja/rituals/sandhi-puja/), the juncture of Ashtami and Navami, are often explained by reference to Rama's offering, and many people link the "victory" of [Bijoya Dashami](/durga-puja/rituals/dashami/) to his triumph over Ravana, which is also celebrated across North India as [Dussehra](/navratri/dussehra-vijayadashami/).

## Spring and autumn: Basanti Puja and Sharadiya

Why call the autumn puja "untimely" at all? Because tradition places the original worship of Durga in **spring**, in the bright fortnight of Chaitra (March or April). Bengali tradition often connects this spring worship to King Suratha, whose story frames the [Devi Mahatmya](/durga-puja/chandi-path/): having lost his kingdom, he learns of the goddess from the sage Medhas and worships her, and many Bengalis remember him as the first to perform Durga Puja. That spring puja is [Basanti Puja](/festivals/basanti-puja/), *basanta* meaning spring. It coincides with Chaitra Navratri elsewhere in India.

The autumn puja, though "out of season", became by far the greater festival in Bengal, the *Sharadiya Durgotsav*. Historians link its growth to the patronage of zamindar families and later to community pujas; see the [history of Durga Puja](/durga-puja/history/). Basanti Puja did not disappear. Some old families and temples still hold it, with the same sequence of rituals on a smaller scale.

## The bodhon today

The story lives on in the ritual calendar. On Shashthi, in 2026 on Saturday 17 October, the priest performs the bodhon, the *amantran* (invitation) and the *adhivas* (preliminary consecration) at a bel tree or a bel branch set up in the pandal, waking the goddess as Rama did. The exact time depends on the tithi, so check your local panjika or the puja committee's notice.
