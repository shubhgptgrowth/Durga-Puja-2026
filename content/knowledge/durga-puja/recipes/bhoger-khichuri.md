Bhoger khichuri is the dish most Bengalis think of first when they think of Durga Puja. It is the warm, soft, golden centre of the bhog plate, the thing volunteers ladle out of huge pots at community pujas on [Maha Ashtami](/durga-puja/rituals/ashtami/), and the dish families cook at home when the puja comes to their own altar. This recipe makes a home-sized pot for four.

## Its place in the puja

*Bhog* is food offered to the goddess before anyone eats it. Khichuri suits that role well: it is a complete, nourishing meal in a single pot, it can be cooked in quantity, and it is made from simple, "pure" ingredients, rice, lentils, ghee and spices, without onion or garlic. Once offered, it becomes *prasad*, and eating it together, seated in rows, is part of the festival. Many pujas serve khichuri on Saptami and [Navami](/durga-puja/rituals/navami/) as well as Ashtami; in 2026, Maha Ashtami falls on Monday 19 October. On the plate it is joined by [labra](/durga-puja/recipes/labra/), [beguni](/durga-puja/recipes/beguni/), [tomato chutney](/durga-puja/recipes/tomato-khejur-chutney/) and [payesh](/durga-puja/recipes/payesh/). See our [Durga Puja food and bhog](/durga-puja/food-and-bhog/) guide for the wider story.

## Tips for success

- **Roast the dal patiently.** Medium-low heat and constant stirring give an even golden colour. If some grains darken to brown, the dal will taste bitter, so start again rather than carry on.
- **Use gobindobhog rice.** This small-grained, naturally fragrant rice from Bengal turns creamy and gives bhoger khichuri its distinctive aroma. You will find it at Indian grocers and online.
- **Use hot water.** Adding cold water to the pot shocks the grains and slows cooking. Keep a kettle of hot water nearby for adjusting the consistency.
- **Stir and scrape.** Khichuri catches easily once it thickens. A heavy-based pot and a flat-edged spoon help.
- **Aim for "flowing" khichuri.** Pandal-style bhoger khichuri is loose enough to spread slowly on the plate, not a stiff mound. It firms up a great deal as it cools.
- **Season with care.** In many homes the cook does not taste bhog before it is offered, so measure the salt rather than guessing. The small amount of sugar is traditional and rounds out the flavour without making it sweet.

## Variations

- **Plain khichuri:** leave out the vegetables entirely for the simplest version, as some family pujas prefer.
- **Seasonal vegetables:** add small cubes of pumpkin, a few pieces of sweet potato or a handful of green beans with the potato.
- **Extra fragrance:** finish with a pinch of ground garam masala or a few cashews and raisins fried in ghee.
- **Hing:** a small pinch of asafoetida in the tempering adds depth, a common addition in no-onion cooking.
- **Fasting days:** khichuri made from rice and lentils is not suitable for every fast; check the [Navratri fasting rules](/navratri/fasting-rules/) or your family's custom.

## Storing and reheating

Khichuri is best eaten within a few hours. Leftovers keep, covered, in the fridge for up to 2 days. It sets firm when cold: reheat gently in a pan with a few tablespoons of hot water per serving, stirring until it loosens, and add a small spoon of ghee. Prasad is usually eaten the same day rather than stored for long.

## Serving

Serve hot, with a spoonful of ghee on top, alongside labra and a crisp beguni. Bhog is traditionally eaten with the right hand from a sal-leaf or steel plate. Leave chutney and payesh for the end of the meal, and if you are serving prasad after a home puja, offer it to guests before family members take their own plates.
