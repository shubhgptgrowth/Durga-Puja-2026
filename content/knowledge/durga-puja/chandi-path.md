**Chandi Path** (*path*, reading or recitation) is the reading aloud of the **Devi Mahatmya**, the *Glory of the Goddess*, as an act of worship. The text is also called the **Durga Saptashati** (*the seven hundred verses to Durga*) or simply the **Chandi**, after one of the goddess's names. It is part of the Markandeya Purana, has 700 verses in 13 chapters, and is usually dated by scholars to around the middle of the first millennium CE. During Durga Puja a priest recites it in the pandal or the family *thakur dalan* (worship hall); during [Navratri](/navratri/) many households read it themselves.

## The frame story

The Devi Mahatmya opens with a human story. **King Suratha** has lost his kingdom, and a merchant named **Samadhi** has been cast out by his greedy family. They meet in the forest hermitage of the sage **Medhas** and ask why they still feel attached to what has hurt them. The sage answers that this is the work of *Mahamaya*, the goddess's power of illusion, and tells them three stories of her greatness. At the end, the two men worship the goddess and receive what they wish for: the king his kingdom, the merchant spiritual knowledge.

## The three episodes

| Episode | Chapters | Story | Presiding form |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Prathama charitra* (first) | 1 | Vishnu, roused from cosmic sleep by the goddess, slays the demons **Madhu and Kaitabha** | Mahakali |
| *Madhyama charitra* (middle) | 2 to 4 | The goddess, born of the gods' radiance, slays **Mahishasura** | Mahalakshmi |
| *Uttama charitra* (final) | 5 to 13 | The goddess defeats **Shumbha and Nishumbha** and their generals, including Chanda, Munda and Raktabija | Mahasaraswati |

Each episode is associated in tradition with a form of the goddess, as in the last column, though all are understood as one supreme goddess. The middle episode is the one shown in the Durga Puja image; read it in full in our [story of Mahishasura](/durga-puja/mahishasura-story/).

The third episode contains two of the most famous hymns: the [Ya Devi Sarvabhuteshu](/durga-puja/rituals/ya-devi-sarvabhuteshu/) litany in chapter 5, and the *Narayani Stuti* in chapter 11, source of the "Sarva mangala mangalye" verse said at [pushpanjali](/durga-puja/rituals/pushpanjali-mantra/). In chapter 7 the fearsome goddess **Kali** springs from the brow of the goddess to destroy Chanda and Munda, earning her the name Chamunda; see the [ten Mahavidyas](/durga-puja/dashamahavidya/) for more on Kali in Bengal.

## The companion texts

A full Chandi Path is rarely the 13 chapters alone. Traditions add hymns before and after, which vary between regions and families, but a common sequence is:

**Before the chapters (the *angas*, limbs):**

- **Devi Kavacham** (the armour): a prayer asking the goddess's forms to protect each part of the body. It includes the well-known list of the nine Durgas, the [Navadurga](/navratri/navadurga/).
- **Argala Stotram** (the bolt): a hymn asking for victory, glory and the removal of enemies, with the refrain *rupam dehi jayam dehi yasho dehi dvisho jahi*. The anjali verse "Om jayanti mangala kali" comes from it.
- **Kilakam** (the pin or key): a short text said to "unlock" the benefit of the recitation.

Many readers also recite the *Ratri Suktam* and a *nyasa* and invocation before beginning, and some include the short *Siddha Kunjika Stotram*.

**After the chapters:**

- **Devi Suktam**: in many traditions the Vedic hymn of Rigveda 10.125, in which the goddess of speech declares herself the power behind all the gods.
- **The three Rahasyas** (secrets): short texts explaining the nature of the goddess's forms.
- **A prayer for forgiveness** (*kshama prarthana*) for any errors in the recitation.

## How families read it during the puja

In Bengal, Chandi Path is part of the priest's duties on each main day of the puja, often done in parallel with the main worship by a second priest. In old family pujas the reading may be done in the *thakur dalan* each morning from [Saptami](/durga-puja/rituals/saptami/) to [Navami](/durga-puja/rituals/navami/). Families sometimes sponsor a full recitation as a vow (*manot*), for a wish or in thanks.

During Navratri, a common household practice is to read the whole text on each of the nine days, or to spread it across the days so that it is completed once. Some people read one *charitra* per day; others follow schemes taught in their family. There is no single correct pattern.

## Respectful guidance for reading at home

These are general suggestions, not rules; your family priest's guidance comes first.

1. **Use a reliable edition.** Choose a printed text with clear Sanskrit and, if helpful, a translation in your language.
2. **Prepare the space.** Clean the area, light a lamp and incense, and place a picture or image of the goddess before you. Keep the book on a stand or a clean cloth rather than on the floor.
3. **Bathe and dress simply** before you begin, and sit on a mat.
4. **Begin with a short prayer** stating your intention (*sankalpa*). If you know the companion hymns, recite them first.
5. **Read clearly and steadily.** It is better to read slowly and correctly than quickly. If you cannot finish in one sitting, finish a chapter or an episode before stopping.
6. **Close with the forgiveness prayer** and an offering of flowers.

Seed-syllable mantras (*bija* mantras) and special vows are traditionally taken under a teacher's guidance. If you are unsure, simple reading with devotion is always appropriate.

## Listening as an alternative

Not everyone can read Sanskrit, and many traditions value *shravana*, attentive listening, as a form of worship. Recordings by well-known reciters are widely available, and many Bengali homes play the Chandi on the radio on [Mahalaya](/durga-puja/rituals/mahalaya/), through the [Mahishasuramardini](/durga-puja/mahishasuramardini-radio/) programme, which in 2026 airs on Saturday 10 October. Listening with a translation open beside you is a good way to learn the story. For simple home worship, see [Durga Puja at home](/durga-puja/at-home/).
