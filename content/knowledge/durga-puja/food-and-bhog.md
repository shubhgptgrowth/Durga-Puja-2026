Durga Puja is as much a food festival as a religious one. At its centre is **bhog**, the meal offered to the goddess and then shared out as blessing; around it, Kolkata eats out for five days straight, queuing at street stalls and old cabins between pandals. This guide covers both, with notes on sweets, family food customs and where to eat on a pandal-hopping night.

## What is bhog?

*Bhog* is food offered to the deity during the puja. Once it has been offered, what remains becomes *prasad*, food carrying the goddess's grace, and it is distributed to devotees. In Bengali community pujas the bhog served to visitors is usually a hot, simple, vegetarian meal, cooked without onion or garlic in many places, and eaten sitting in long rows on the floor or at trestle tables, often from leaf plates or *shal pata* (sal-leaf plates).

The classic Durga Puja bhog plate looks like this:

| Dish | What it is |
|---|---|
| Khichuri | Rice and lentils (often roasted moong dal) cooked together with ghee and whole spices; the heart of the plate |
| Labra | A mixed vegetable dish of pumpkin, potato, radish, aubergine and greens, cooked soft with *panch phoron* (five-spice) |
| Beguni | Aubergine slices dipped in gram-flour batter and fried; other *bhaja* (fries) may be served too |
| Chutney | Sweet and sour, commonly tomato, sometimes with dates or *amsotto* (dried mango) |
| Payesh | Rice slow-cooked in milk with sugar, a sweet finish |

Some pujas serve plain rice, dal and a mixed vegetable *chorchori* instead of khichuri, or add *papad* and a sweet. Bonedi bari (old family) pujas often have their own long-standing menus, including offerings such as *luchi* (fried flatbread), fruit and home-made sweets. The menu genuinely varies, so treat the table above as typical rather than fixed.

### Why bhog is shared

The sharing is the point. Food offered to the goddess is not kept back; it goes out to whoever has come, without asking who they are. In a *sarbojanin* (community, "for everyone") puja, people of every background sit down side by side for the same khichuri. Many families remember Ashtami bhog at their para pandal as one of the most important meals of the year. If you are offered bhog, accept it with your right hand, eat what you take and don't waste it.

## Pujo eating culture: Kolkata's streets

Outside the pandal, Durga Puja is a city-wide night out. Stalls set up along pandal routes, restaurants extend their hours, and old *cabins* (small, decades-old cafés) see queues. Favourites you will see everywhere:

- **Phuchka**: hollow crisp shells filled with spiced potato and tamarind water, eaten one at a time from the vendor's hand. Kolkata's take on pani puri.
- **Egg roll and chicken roll**: a paratha fried with egg, filled with onion, chilli and meat or just egg, rolled in paper. Kolkata's rolls are a pujo staple.
- **Mughlai paratha**: a stuffed, egg-filled paratha fried on a flat griddle, cut into squares and served with potato curry and salad.
- **Kabiraji**: a cutlet of fish or chicken coated in a lacy, crisp web of beaten egg, a speciality of old cabins.
- **Fish fry and cutlets**: breaded fish, chops and *devilled eggs* (*dimer devil*).
- **Kolkata biryani**: famous for its potato and boiled egg alongside the meat; many people plan one biryani dinner during the puja.
- **Ghugni, jhalmuri and telebhaja**: chickpea curry, spiced puffed rice and fritters, cheap and fast between pandals.

Two classic cabins you can fit into a pandal walk: [Mitra Cafe at Sovabazar](/guide/food/mitra_cafe/), close to the north Kolkata heritage pujas, and the [Indian Coffee House on College Street](/guide/food/coffee_house/), near the central theme pandals. The full [Kolkata guide](/guide/) lists eateries by area, near each pandal.

## Sweets: the taste of Pujo

No Bengali festival is complete without *mishti* (sweets). Boxes of sweets are bought for guests, carried on visits and above all exchanged on Bijoya Dashami.

- **Rosogolla**: soft spongy balls of *chhana* (fresh cottage cheese) in light syrup.
- **Sandesh**: a drier chhana sweet, pressed into moulds, sometimes flavoured with cardamom, mango or saffron.
- **Mishti doi**: sweetened, caramelised yoghurt set in clay pots.
- **Pantua, chomchom, langcha, rajbhog**: fried or syrup-soaked chhana sweets, each with its own texture.
- **Narkel naru**: coconut balls, often made at home and offered at the puja and on Bijoya.

A note on **nolen gur**: this "new" date-palm jaggery, used in prized sweets like nolen gurer sandesh, is collected in the cold months, so its season normally comes after Durga Puja. You may see gur-flavoured sweets in October, but the fresh-gur season is winter.

## Family food customs during the puja

Food customs vary a great deal from family to family, so none of the following applies everywhere:

- **Vegetarian days.** Many households eat *niramish* (vegetarian, often without onion and garlic) food on some puja days, particularly Ashtami, the day of [Pushpanjali](/durga-puja/rituals/pushpanjali-mantra/), when people often fast until offering flowers in the morning.
- **Navami mutton.** In many Bengali families Navami is associated with a special mutton or meat meal, and some families link this to the tradition of *bali* (ritual sacrifice) in older pujas. Other families do not follow this at all. See the [Navami page](/durga-puja/rituals/navami/) for the day's rituals.
- **Dashami.** Families commonly prepare or buy sweets to share after the immersion, when people visit relatives for Bijoya greetings. Read more in our guide to [Bijoya Dashami wishes](/durga-puja/bijoya-dashami-wishes/).

## Eating well on a pandal-hopping night

- Eat a proper meal before a long walk; food lines at big pandals can be as long as entry lines.
- Choose busy stalls where food is cooked fresh in front of you, and carry your own drinking water.
- Keep cash and UPI both ready; small stalls may prefer one or the other.
- Late at night, many popular eateries near the big pandals stay open, but don't count on it everywhere.
