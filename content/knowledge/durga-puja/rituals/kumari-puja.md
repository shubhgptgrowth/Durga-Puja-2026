**Kumari Puja** (*kumari*, maiden) is the worship of a young girl as a living form of the goddess. During Durga Puja in Bengal it is most often held on the morning of **Maha Ashtami**, which in 2026 falls on **Monday 19 October**. The best-known public Kumari Puja is at **Belur Math**, the headquarters of the Ramakrishna Math and Mission on the Hooghly opposite Kolkata.

## The idea behind it

Shakta tradition teaches that the goddess is present in every woman, and that she can be worshipped in a girl as purely as in an image. The young girl, before puberty, stands for the goddess's innocence and creative power in its unformed, potential state. Worshipping her is meant to teach devotees to see the divine in every woman, and to honour girls and women in everyday life.

Tantric and Puranic texts describe Kumari Puja, and some give the goddess a different name for each age of the girl. Practice varies, and not every Durga Puja includes it.

## What happens

Details differ by tradition, but the sequence usually looks like this:

1. The chosen girl is bathed and dressed in a **red sari**, with flowers, ornaments and sometimes a crown.
2. Her feet are washed and decorated with *alta* (red dye), and she is seated on a decorated seat near the deity.
3. The priest worships her as the goddess, with the same mantras and offerings used for Durga: flowers, bel leaves, lamps, incense, fruit, sweets and cooked food.
4. Devotees, including monks and priests, bow before her.

The girl is treated as the goddess for the duration of the ritual, and families often describe it as a moment of great honour.

## Belur Math and Swami Vivekananda

Kumari Puja was practised in Bengal long before modern times, but its best-known public form today is at Belur Math. **Swami Vivekananda** is widely credited with holding the first Durga Puja at Belur Math in 1901 and worshipping young girls as the goddess there, reflecting the teaching of his guru Sri Ramakrishna, who worshipped his wife Sri Sarada Devi as the goddess. The Ramakrishna Math has continued the tradition, and on Ashtami morning large crowds gather at Belur Math to see it. Check the Math's own announcements for that year's arrangements.

Some other temples and community pujas in Bengal, and in other parts of India, also hold Kumari Puja.

## Kumari Puja and kanya pujan

In much of North and West India, families mark Ashtami or Navami of [Navratri](/navratri/) with [kanya pujan](/navratri/kanya-pujan/), inviting several young girls home, washing their feet and serving them food and gifts. Both share the same idea, the goddess present in a girl, but Kumari Puja in Bengal is usually a fuller temple or pandal ritual centred on one girl.

## Visiting

- Belur Math is across the river from North Kolkata and is crowded on Ashtami morning; go early and follow the volunteers' instructions.
- Photography rules differ; ask before taking pictures of the Kumari.
- Combine it with the Ashtami [pushpanjali](/durga-puja/rituals/pushpanjali-mantra/), and read about [Maha Ashtami](/durga-puja/rituals/ashtami/) and the [goddess Durga](/durga-puja/goddess-durga/).
